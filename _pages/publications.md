with Anne Ardila Brenøe
<details><summary>Abstract</summary> -->
We study how adolescents' second-order beliefs about their parents' occupational preferences shape gendered career aspirations. In a consequential early-career choice setting, we combine a parental choice experiment with a randomized salience intervention among students. Parents give gendered recommendations, but students substantially overestimate fathers' preference for boys to choose male-dominated occupations as well as mothers' preference for girls to choose female-dominated occupations. Making the same-gender parent salient raises aspirations for gender-congruent occupations, while highlighting the opposite-gender parent and both parents has no effect. Salience does not shift perceived occupational fit, suggesting that identity-based second-order beliefs can reinforce occupational gender segregation.
</details>

<details><summary><strong><a href="/files/Reducing_Emissions_in_the_Laboratory.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Reducing Emissions in the Laboratory</a></strong><br>with Nils Handler and Nicolás Harrington Ruiz</summary>
Laboratory sustainability programs have been widely adopted, yet evidence for their effects rests on uncontrolled before-and-after comparisons. We provide the first causal evidence, using a randomized controlled trial with 109 research groups at the University of Zurich. The intervention, adapted from a program used by over 150 institutions, combines practice guidelines, personalized energy-use feedback, and certification. We find no detectable effect on energy use and rule out reductions larger than 14%, including those the program reports. Most groups already met the program's entry-level certification standard at baseline, suggesting such programs may largely recognize existing practices rather than change them.
</details>

## Work in Progress

<details><summary><strong>Men in Care Jobs</strong> <br> with Anne Ardila Brenøe, Natalie Irmert, and Louisa Roos</summary>
Demand for care workers is rising, yet men remain starkly underrepresented in care occupations. Drawing on original surveys of adolescents, data from a large apprenticeship platform, longitudinal administrative education records, and the Swiss Labour Force Survey, we study why few boys choose care in Switzerland's vocational education system. We provide a descriptive analysis of who selects into care, what beliefs and misperceptions may underlie the gender gap, and how men in care fare over time, with lessons for policies to diversify the care workforce.
</details>
